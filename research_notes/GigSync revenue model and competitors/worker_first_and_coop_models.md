## Key Question 1: Which models have reached sustainability, at what scale, and with which paying party?

### Takeaway

As of October 2026 no "no-commission" gig platform in the sample has published a profit. The closest to break-even are the ones where a third party pays a flat per-placement or per-month fee (Haqdarshak: ₹40.1 Cr revenue, ₹0.76 Cr loss FY25; Nia.one: ₹30 Cr ARR, profitability undisclosed), or where the platform is a thin layer over a worker-owned business (Up & Go 5–8% of job value, Drivers Cooperative NYC within $318k of break-even on $5.9M in 2022). Worker-subscription ride-hailing (Namma Yatri) has scale but lost ₹51 Cr on ₹20–24 Cr revenue in FY25; government-run clones (Yatri Sathi, Kerala Savaari, Bharat Taxi) publish no P&L at all.

## Key Question 2: Where the customer pays, what is the price point and uptake? Where a third party pays, what exactly do they buy?

### Takeaway

Customer-pays models cluster around either a small per-job add-on ($5 Alia, 5–8% Up & Go, 22% Hilfr platform share, ~22.5% TaskRabbit) or a monthly subscription (~$39 Care.com). Uptake data exists only where contributions were voluntary and the scheme failed (Alia). Third parties buy specific deliverables: CSR/government buys completed scheme applications and data (Haqdarshak), enterprises buy staffing placements at 5% (Nia.one) or employee back-up care (Care.com Enterprise, +18% in Q4 2024), governments buy a public ride-hailing utility (ONDC/Juspay stack for Yatri Sathi; ITI Ltd for Kerala Savaari), and foundations buy research and litigation capacity (Fairwork, WIE, Driver's Seat).

### Cited Findings

- Hilfr: customer pays ~DKK 429/hr of which 22% is platform/insurance/operations, 58% to cleaner, 20% VAT; Danish servicefradrag tax deduction lowers effective customer cost to ~DKK 363/hr (site © 2026) — [hilfr.dk](https://hilfr.dk/en)

### Inferences

- Where the customer pays an explicit add-on on top of the worker's price (Hilfr 22%, TaskRabbit ~22.5%), the "no commission" framing is cosmetic: the load on the transaction is comparable to a commission; the difference is who sees it on the invoice and whether the worker's rate is protected.
- Voluntary customer contributions (Alia) did not survive; Care.com's consumer subscriptions are shrinking while employer-paid contracts grow. The paying party that is growing in the sample is the enterprise/CSR/government buyer, not the household.

## Key Question 3: What do worker subscriptions cost and what share of workers actually pay? (Namma Yatri as the key case)

## Key Question 4: What do portable-benefits schemes (Alia, Hilfr, SMart) charge and who contributes?

### Takeaway

Three different contributors: Alia asked the client for a voluntary $5 per cleaning (failed, closed March 2022); Hilfr builds pension (12.15%, employer 10.15%), holiday pay and sickness supplements into a customer price where the platform keeps 22% and the worker 58%, under a 3F collective agreement; SMart takes 6.5% of each member's invoice and runs members as salaried employees so statutory Belgian social security applies. Only the two European models, which make benefits compulsory and price them into what the customer pays, have survived.

### Inferences

- The portable-benefit designs that lasted are the ones where contributions are not optional: Hilfr via collective agreement and price structure, SMart via employment status. Alia's voluntary client contribution is the design that failed.
- Namma Yatri's "2% of revenue" welfare trust would be ~₹40–47 lakh on FY25 revenue (own arithmetic), i.e. under ₹10 per registered driver per year.

## Key Question 5: What killed the ones that failed (Driver's Seat, Gigbox, Rodeo, Alia, Indian platform co-ops)?

### Takeaway

The failures share one pattern: no paying party. Driver's Seat sold data insights that cities and researchers used but (on the evidence) did not pay for, and wound down when its Ford/Rockefeller grants ran out; Alia relied on voluntary client contributions and was shut in March 2022; Rodeo lost its data feed when Deliveroo blocked it and has gone silent since 2023; Kerala Savaari v1 lost drivers to better-paying private apps and could not keep enough vehicles online. The Drivers Cooperative NYC survived only by pivoting from consumer ride-hail to government paratransit contracts.

### Inferences

- Data cooperatives (Driver's Seat, Gigbox, Rodeo) failed on two fronts: platforms control the data pipe and can cut it off (Deliveroo/Rodeo), and the natural buyers (cities, researchers, unions) consume data without paying for it. Grants bridged this for 2–4 years and then stopped.
- The Indian "co-op" failure case is not a worker-owned co-op (none has been found to have closed) but a government-run subscription app (Kerala Savaari v1) that could not match private-platform driver earnings and never reached the vehicle density a rider needs. The 2.0 relaunch answers this with a central PSU (ITI) as tech partner rather than a new revenue source.
- Bharat Taxi is structurally the same bet (government-backed, flat daily fee) at 10x the scale and with a ₹300 Cr-class corpus; it has not yet published anything that would show whether the ₹18–30/day fee covers operations.

- No primary statement from Driver's Seat on why it closed, nor its revenue (if any) from data sales.
- Rodeo's current legal status (Companies House) not checked.
- No Indian worker-owned platform co-op shutdown was found in the press; this may reflect absence of coverage rather than absence of failures.
- Documented's original Oct 2024 article on the Drivers Cooperative was not retrieved; the findings are via The Hustle's summary.
