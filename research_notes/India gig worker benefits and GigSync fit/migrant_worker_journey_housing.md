# Migrant gig worker journey, housing and support in India (as of 27 Oct 2026)

Scope: inter-state / inter-district migrants doing delivery, ride-hail / bike-taxi, home-service and domestic / beauty work in Indian metros. Every row marks the source type: **[Evidence]** = survey, government data, company disclosure, or court/police record; **[Anecdote]** = single-case reporting, viral post, or vendor blog. Dates are the publication/measurement date of the source. Tool-call budget was exhausted before several primary documents could be opened; those are listed under Gaps rather than paraphrased from memory.

## KQ1. What share of delivery / ride / home-service gig workers in the big metros are migrants, and from where?

### Takeaway

The best available national sample (IDinsight, Mar 2025, n=2,547 two-wheeler delivery drivers) puts migrants at roughly half of the delivery workforce, split almost evenly between intra-state and inter-state; a Bengaluru-specific ISEC brief (Dec 2025) finds the migrant delivery pool there is dominated by _intra-state_ movers from north Karnataka (66%) with only ~14% from other states. There is no city-by-city origin table for Delhi/Mumbai/Hyderabad gig workers specifically; general migrant-origin data (UP/Bihar/Jharkhand for Delhi) has to stand in.

### Inferences

- "Migrant" in Bengaluru's delivery fleet mostly means a Kannada-speaking intra-state mover, while Delhi-NCR and Kerala fleets skew to long-distance Hindi/Bengali/Assamese speakers. A worker-first app cannot assume a single language or that "home" is another state; the intra-state migrant (short trips home, same ration/bank state) and the inter-state migrant (ONORC, language, Aadhaar address) have different needs.
- Short tenure (IDinsight ~13 months; ISEC "most joined ~a year ago") means the onboarding/first-weeks problem recurs constantly; the population churns, so acquisition cost matters.
- The ~₹18.7k net / 62 h week figure sets the affordability ceiling for everything downstream (rent, vehicle rent, remittance).

### Gaps

- No study found that reports migrant share + origin state for gig workers in Delhi, Mumbai, Hyderabad or Pune specifically. Janpahal (Delhi) and NCAER/ILO/CIS/Aapti reports were not located in search results within budget.
- No Fairwork India 2025 ratings surfaced in two searches; as of 27 Oct 2026 the 2024 edition is the latest I could confirm.
- The ISEC PDF downloaded but no PDF text extractor was available locally (no pdftotext/pypdf), so sample size and method are unverified; the 66/20/14 split comes from the search-engine snippet of that document.
- No data on migrant share among home-service / beauty professionals (Urban Company) or ride-hail drivers in metros.

## KQ2. Housing: how migrant gig workers find rooms, what beds cost, and what formal options exist

### Takeaway

Migrant workers find rooms almost entirely through origin-based social networks and village landlords in informal rental markets (~25% of India's housing stock), with no contracts; costs run from ~₹500/month for a windowless slum room (Mumbai, anecdote) to ₹8,000–15,000/bed for formal PGs that are priced for white-collar tenants. The government's ARHC scheme funded projects only to March 2022, the official portal's TLS certificate had expired on 27 Oct 2026, and the one named blue-collar co-living startup (Tikaana) has no post-2021 coverage; platform "rest points" (Zomato 5,000+, Swiggy Instamart 900+) are daytime shelters, not sleeping accommodation.

### Inferences

- The gap between what a delivery worker nets (~₹18.7k) and what a formal PG bed costs (₹8k+) explains why the informal, no-contract, village-landlord market dominates; a "rent a bed" product has to price at ₹2–4k/bed to be relevant, which is below every formal operator found.
- No evidence of any platform (Zomato/Swiggy/Rapido/Urban Company) offering lodging or housing help; their investment stops at daytime rest points.
- The B2B employer-pays model (Tikaana with Delhivery/Quess) is the only blue-collar housing model found; it does not reach independent gig contractors, who have no employer to pay.

### Gaps

- No source found for per-bed rent paid by _gig workers specifically_ in any metro; all price data is either formal PG (too expensive) or one viral anecdote.
- Tikaana's current operations, pricing and funding after 2021 could not be confirmed; a direct search on the company returned nothing relevant.
- ARHC delivery numbers (units completed, occupancy) could not be read because the official portal's certificate had expired.
- No evidence found of any startup marketing "rider hostels" or bed rental to delivery riders; the searches surfaced only traveller hostels (The Hosteller) and student PG aggregators (Homversity).
- Urban Company housing assistance: nothing found.

## KQ4. Documents and money: Aadhaar address, SIM, bank/UPI, police verification, DL/vehicle rental, e-Shram, ONORC. Which steps block onboarding and for how long?

### Takeaway

The legal scaffolding for a migrant to bank and register at a new address exists (RBI 2016 KYC rule, 2019 PMLA self-declaration for a non-Aadhaar address, e-Shram with ONORC integrated since Oct 2024), but reporting shows banks still refuse accounts over address proof and the worker bears the burden of updating Aadhaar. Platform registrations on e-Shram jumped from 3.37 lakh (Aug 2025) to ~11.5 lakh (Aug 2026), still a small fraction of the 12 million gig workforce. No source gave the number of days any document step delays onboarding.

- The helpline infrastructure is optimised for a contractor-employer wage dispute with a phone number to call; a platform worker's disputes (ID block, payout deduction, kit deduction, customer violence) have no comparable mediator, which is a gap a worker-first app could route rather than solve.
- Job-fee scams exploit the same recruiter-marketplace layer (Job Hai brand misuse) that genuinely onboards delivery workers, so "verify the recruiter" is a concrete, cheap feature.

### Gaps

- No evidence found of gig-specific deposit scams (fake Zomato/Swiggy onboarding agents charging fees); searches returned only customer-side scams. Absence of reporting is not absence of practice.
- No data on contractor/"seth" cuts for gig workers (fleet-owner models in ride-hail were not researched within budget).
- India Labourline's current phone number and 2024–26 case totals not found (site counters empty).

## KQ8. What NGOs and governments have built for migrant workers, and how much is usable by a gig worker

### Takeaway

The NGO toolset (Aajeevika's walk-in Shramik Sahayata Kendras in Rajasthan/Gujarat/Maharashtra, Jan Sahas's Migrants Resilience Collaborative, Gram Vaani's IVR networks, India Labourline) is built for construction, manufacturing and garment migrants and their wage/entitlement problems; the government layer (e-Shram with ONORC, state gig bills) is registration-centric. Nothing found is designed around the platform worker's actual journey (room, vehicle, kit, ID activation, payouts), and Haqdarshak's gig-worker work did not surface in search.

### Inferences

- Every existing migrant-support asset is anchored to a _sector_ (construction, garments) or a _corridor_ (south Rajasthan → Gujarat), not to a _journey_. A worker-first app that sequences the journey (arrive → room → documents → vehicle → platform ID → first payout → remit) would be routing to these assets rather than competing with them, and would find no incumbent at the arrival-and-housing step.
- The Kerala finding that awareness of existing migrant schemes is low, together with e-Shram's registration-without-benefits pattern, suggests "scheme discovery and claim" (the Haqdarshak model) is a plausible monetisable layer, but I could not confirm Haqdarshak's current gig-worker offering.

### Gaps

- Haqdarshak: no results on gig-worker products or partnerships within the searches run.
- Shramik Sahayata Kendra current count and whether any serves platform workers: not confirmed beyond 2021 figures.
- No evaluation found of ONORC uptake by inter-state migrants in 2025–26.
- No evidence found on Jan Sahas or MRC digital tools/apps.
